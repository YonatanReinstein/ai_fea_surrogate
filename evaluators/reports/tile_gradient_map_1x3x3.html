Reading the map

Three checks the solver was never told to satisfy

0.17% / 0.9% / 7.1%

Mirror symmetry

Geometry and load are symmetric about y = 10.5, so d1/d3, d4/d6 and d7/d9 have to pair. They do, to within those margins — and nothing in the pipeline imposes it. It falls out of three independent ccx solves. The pairs widen as the magnitude drops, which is the signature of finite-difference noise rather than of a broken gradient.

−2.73e8 vs −3.05e8

The neutral axis shows up

The load bends the plate about x, putting the neutral axis at y = 10.5 — exactly the d2/d5/d8 column. Those tiles should do the least for bending stiffness, and at the root d2 is the weakest of the three despite sitting directly under the load path. Away from the root the same effect flips the sign outright: d5 and d8 go positive.

≈660×

Root dominance

The strongest root tile is −3.05e+08 against the strongest tip tile’s −4.59e+05. All the bending moment is at z = 0, so essentially the entire optimisation gradient lives in the three root tiles — the row sums are −8.8e+08 at the root against +6.7e+04 at the tip. A surrogate that gets the tip tiles wrong costs almost nothing; one that gets the root row wrong is useless.

Mesh convergence

5,5,5 is not enough for tiles this large

Same nine tiles, same colour scale, three resolutions. At 5,5,5 the whole field is too weak and d5/d8 point the wrong way. At 7,7,7 and 9,9,9 the structure is stable. This is the elements-per-wall rule: nine tiles over the same plate makes each element roughly 2.3× coarser in plane than the 48-tile geometry, and the 0.3-thick frame walls stop being resolved.
